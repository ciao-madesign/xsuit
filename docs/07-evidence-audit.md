# 07 — Evidence audit

> Generated by `npm run evidence` from `rules/`. Do not edit by hand.

Every rule must be traceable to a real study or to stated observations, with its limits. This page lists each rule, what supports it and what the evidence does **not** show.

**Levels:** strong = two or more independent studies/reviews; moderate = at least one study/review; emerging = indirect or proxy measures; hypothesis = observation or design guess, to be validated (a validation plan is mandatory).

**Totals:** 18 rules: 0 strong, 6 moderate, 7 emerging, 5 hypothesis.

## Rules

### `visual.china.compact-density` — moderate

- **Action:** Use a compact layout density with more content per screen. (china, visual, risk low)
- **Why:** Chinese homepages show about three times the visual density and nearly twice the main content blocks; NN/G reports Chinese users prefer relatively high information density and perceive complexity through familiarity.
- **Supported by:**
  - `li2026`, Section 2.2 table: visual density 38.21 vs 12.87; main content blocks 5.91 vs 3.14 — Higher density and more blocks on Chinese homepages
  - `nng2016`, Section 2.3: Chinese users prefer relatively high information density — Preference for density (supporting)
- **Does not show:**
  - Evidence comes from institutional (municipal) homepages; commercial sites are not covered.
  - Preference evidence is not from a controlled study of this transformation.
  - Both user groups struggle when complexity becomes excessive (NN/G).
- **Limits:** Tendency from research, not a universal rule; varies by site and sector. Touch targets must stay usable.
- **Test results:** none yet

### `visual.china.tight-spacing` — moderate

- **Action:** Reduce spacing between blocks. (china, visual, risk low)
- **Why:** The literature review reports less dominant white space on Chinese sites, consistent with denser layouts; doc 01 section 3.3 lists white space as less dominant.
- **Supported by:**
  - `li2022`, Section 2.1: Chinese sites have more elements and interactions; Western sites more white space — Less white space in Chinese designs
  - `project-synthesis`, Section 3.3 table: white space less dominant — Descriptive comparison
- **Does not show:**
  - Methods and definitions differ across the reviewed studies.
  - No test of whether tighter spacing helps users.
- **Limits:** Tendency from research, not a universal rule; varies by site and sector.
- **Test results:** none yet

### `visual.china.vivid-palette` — moderate

- **Action:** Increase colour saturation for a livelier look. (china, visual, risk low)
- **Why:** The 2026 study finds Chinese palettes wider, more saturated and brighter, and the 2022 review reports more red and blue. Doc 02 warns against the red+gold stereotype, so this rule changes saturation only, not hue.
- **Supported by:**
  - `li2026`, Section 2.2 other results: Chinese palettes wider, more saturated, brighter — Saturation difference
  - `li2022`, Section 2.1: more red and blue on Chinese sites — Colour usage difference
- **Does not show:**
  - Evidence comes from institutional (municipal) homepages; commercial sites are not covered.
  - No evidence that raising saturation improves experience.
  - Hue is deliberately untouched.
- **Limits:** Tendency from research, not a universal rule; varies by site and sector. Must keep text contrast at or above WCAG AA. Never apply a red/gold stereotype.
- **Test results:** none yet

### `visual.western.spacious-density` — moderate

- **Action:** Use a spacious layout density with more whitespace. (western, visual, risk low)
- **Why:** Western homepages in the sample show about a third of the visual density of Chinese ones and fewer content blocks; NN/G reports Western users prefer visual simplicity. Raising whitespace moves a dense page toward that convention without removing content.
- **Supported by:**
  - `li2026`, Section 2.2 table: visual density 12.87 vs 38.21 per 1,000 px; main content blocks 3.14 vs 5.91 — Lower density and fewer blocks on Western homepages
  - `nng2016`, Section 2.3: Western users prefer visual simplicity — Preference for simplicity (supporting)
- **Does not show:**
  - Evidence comes from institutional (municipal) homepages; commercial sites are not covered.
  - Preference evidence is not from a controlled study of this transformation.
  - Search-results and article pages are extrapolations from homepages.
- **Limits:** Tendency from research, not a universal rule; varies by site and sector. Only spacing changes; content must not be hidden.
- **Test results:** none yet

### `visual.western.underlined-links` — moderate

- **Action:** Make links visibly underlined so they read as links. (western, visual, risk low)
- **Why:** The literature review reports more underlined links on Western sites. Underlining is also a well-established affordance, and it keeps links identifiable without relying on colour alone.
- **Supported by:**
  - `li2022`, Section 2.1: Western sites have more underlined links — Underlined links more frequent in the West
- **Does not show:**
  - Frequency of a design choice, not evidence that it improves usability for a given user.
  - Methods differ across the reviewed studies.
- **Limits:** Tendency from research, not a universal rule; varies by site and sector. Must not underline buttons or navigation chrome.
- **Test results:** none yet

  - `li2026`, Section 2.2 table: main content blocks 5.91 vs 3.14 — More parallel blocks
  - `project-synthesis`, docs/02 visual level: hierarchy distributed across modules — Interpretation

- **Validation plan:** Internal before/after review (B8) on the paired test sites; add real-user testing after V1.
- **Limits:** Tendency from research, not a universal rule; varies by site and sector. Distributed hierarchy is not absence of hierarchy; keep headings and semantics intact.
- **Test results:** none yet
